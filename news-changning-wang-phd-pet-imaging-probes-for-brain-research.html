Changning Wang, PHD: PET Imaging Probes for Brain Research

2026-01-05

Changning Wang, PHD

Associate Professor of Radiology
Athinoula A. Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging
Department of Radiology
Massachusetts General Hospital
Harvard Medical School

Abstract

Non-invasive molecular imaging methods, e.g. PET, have been developed to visualize and analyze biochemistry-based molecular targets in inaccessible tissues, e.g. the brain, and therefore provided distinct advantages for brain research. The methods are in contrast with in vitro techniques that cannot provide analyses of systems level intact organisms over time in endogenous environments. Notably, in vivo molecular PET imaging has been developed as an important tool to evaluate disease pathological progresses and stages. The PET ligands, such as those for Aβ plaques and NFTs, are widely utilized and becoming more routine in clinical evaluation of AD and trials of potential drugs. They have advanced the development of anti-amyloid antibodies in the clinic. These imaging probes can be used as imaging biomarkers, enabling better definition of optimal patient groups for clinical trials. In drug clinical trials, PET could be used to measure biodistribution by directly radiolabeling drug candidates and measure drug target occupancy, which is a key parameter for clinical success, to establish a link between target occupancy and pharmacological effect in vivo. Such PET occupancy studies are only possible when a PET probe is available for imaging the target of interest as well as sharing the same binding sites with the drugs to be tested. Although some established PET tracers are available for targets in the CNS, probes are needed and as an integrated part of the drug development program and it needs to start with the successful development of new PET probes for the targets of interests and validated in humans before using it for target occupancy study. I will talk about how to develop PET probes and their applications in brain research, with some examples for my research projects.

Short Bio

Changning received his PhD degree in molecular imaging at the Case Western Reserve University, and postdoctoral training at MGH. He is currently an Associate Professor of Radiology at Harvard Medical School, faculty of the Athinoula A. Martinos Center at MGH, Center for Interdisciplinary Innovations in Imaging at MGH and Department of Radiology at BWH. He has a multidisciplinary training background in molecular imaging, chemistry, pharmaceutical sciences and neuroscience. His research specializes in the development and application of novel imaging probes for preclinical and clinical imaging investigations of human disease mechanisms and drug development, which might facilitate understanding of basic neurobiological causes or contributions to brain disorders. He has developed several new imaging probes for PET imaging for brain targets such as protein aggregates, epigenetics and neuroinflammation and led clinical trials with new PET imaging probes.
